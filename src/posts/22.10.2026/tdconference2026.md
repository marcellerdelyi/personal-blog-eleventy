Hello again, its been a while!

Today, I wanted to share something different from my usual technical deep dives and case studies. Earlier this week, on 19 October, I got to volunteer at Trondheim Developer Conference, aka TDC!

This was my first time ever attending the conference, and oh boy, what an experience it was!

If you’re unfamiliar with it, TDC has been held annually since 2012 in Trondheim, a city often referred to as Norway’s tech capital. The conference is mainly run by passionate volunteers from Trondheim’s tech community, who invite experts from the city, the rest of Norway, and abroad to speak at the event.

# The road to TDC

My involvement with the conference started on a hot summer day while I was on holiday in Hungary. I was casually scrolling through LinkedIn, when I stumbled across a post saying: “Bli frivillig på byens triveligste teknologikonferanse” which I’d translate into English as something like: “Volunteer at the city’s most vibrant tech conference” or something along those lines.

Translating words like "trivelig" into English is a bit tricky. Even after living in this beautiful country for the past eight years, I still have plenty to learn, haha.

But anyhow, I decided to step outside my usual comfort zone and send in an application. I still feel like a bit of a baby dev, finding my feet in the software world and trying to find my place in the community. Here was a chance to meet people who share my interests, learn something new, and help make the conference happen. So joining as a volunteer sounded like a no-brainer to me!

And oh my, was I excited when I got an email in September saying that I’d been selected!

{% image "./TDC_Acceptence_Letter.jpg", "Accaptance e-mail from the TDC committee" %}
_📸 Acceptance e-mail from the TDC committee_

Our first meetup was a week before the conference. We got an introduction to the organisation, how it’s funded, and the work that goes into making the event happen.

I hadn’t realised that the committee members do all of this for free too. Finding speakers, coordinating partners(sponsors), recruiting us and other helpers, all of this takes months of planning.

We also learned how TDC supports smaller tech events and activities in Trondheim. Any surplus that the conference makes goes back into supporting the local tech community, helping to fund meetups and other gatherings throughout the year. The remaining money goes back to finance next year’s TDC.

After the presentaion and intro, we had pizza and a chance to chit-chat and get to know each other better.

We met once more on Sunday, just a day before TDC, right on location at the Clarion conference Hotel in Trodnheim to familiarise ourselves with the place and find our way around. We also briefly went through the schedule and got our shirts and conference tickets. We wrapped up the meetup with great conversations and even more pizza. Everything was now in place for the big day!

# TDC 2026

{% image "./TDC_ConferenceDay.png", "Map of the conference and the volunteers’ morning schedule, showing each station." %}
_📸 Map of the conference and the volunteers’ morning schedule_

We started the day quite early, roughly 20 minutes before the first guests arrived. As volunteers, our main jobs were to welcome attendees, handle check-ins, provide room support during talks, and be there for anyone who needed help throughout the conference.

{% image "./55603158176_2b629e6878_o.jpg", "My fellow volunteers welcoming attendees at the entrance and handing out day passes." %}
_📸 Photos by LYKT © by the TDC Committee - My fellow volunteers welcoming attendees at the entrance and handing out day passes._

I was assigned to the room support group. We welcomed participants, helped them find the right rooms, and kept the doors closed during sessions to minimise disruptions.

After each talk, we collected feedback using poker chips. We handed them out for people to drop into one of two voting bags, marked with a 👍 and 👎 symbol, depending on how they felt about the talk. We also had to keep the crowd moving to prevent queues near the doors.

{% image "./Voting_feedback_Counting.png", "Voting sacks containing the physical poker chips’ morning schedule, showing each station." %}
_📸 Illustration of our physical voting system, collecting feedback during the conference_

My first shift was for the conference’s opening talk in Cosmos, the largest room. It was absolutely packed!

The talk was “After the AI Hype” by Richard Campbell. Richard walked us through the past decade of AI, from how OpenAI got started to the companies and capabilities we see today. He also shared his thoughts on where it might all be heading next.

Once the talk wrapped up, it was our turn to get busy handing out voting chips and keeping the crowd moving. I hadn’t quite realised what a packed room would mean for us at the doors! Judging by my fellow volunteers’ reactions, I don’t think it was just me. The first, let’s say, 20 people slipped past us before we got into the swing of things, but we managed to collect plenty of feedback from the others.

I have to say, things went a lot more smoothly after that first talk. By the end of the conference, I’d even dare say we’d perfected our routine for handling the crowd and collecting votes, haha.

You might ask: why not collect the feedback digitally in 2026?

Well, I was asking the same question and raising an eyebrow at the committee when I first heard about it. But after seeing how quickly people left the rooms between talks, I could understand the reasoning. Dropping a chip into a bag on your way out takes almost no time. I suspect we’d have collected a lot less feedback if people had needed to stop and fill something out on their phones.

The results still ended up online, though. After each talk, we counted the chips and entered the totals into a digital form for the organisers and speakers, helping them see whether the talks resonated with people or needed a little tuning.

{% image "./55603283418_ef9a0d7aec_o.jpg", "Crowd at the Stands area" %}
_📸 Photos by LYKT © by the TDC Committee - Crowd mingling at the stands area_

Many of the talks focused on practical uses of AI, and I really enjoyed seeing what people were doing with it. But Christian Breivik’s “AtB-appen låser seg” ended up being my favourite. And that one had nothing to do with AI!

In about 20 minutes, Christian walked us through a problem with AtB, one of Norway’s largest bus apps, that affected just 0.02% of its users. Funnily enough, the fix itself was quite simple. But in tracking it down, they’d also managed to squeeze a lot more performance out of the app. A pretty nice side effect of fixing a bug!

{% image "./20261019_124056.jpg", "My favourite talk from Christian Breivik “AtB-appen låser sega" %}
_📸 My favourite talk from Christian Breivik “AtB-appen låser seg"_

The only real downside I picked up on from participants regarding the talks, was a bit of FOMO. With several talks happening at once, people were rushing to catch the ones they wanted to see.

During my breaks, I overheard people chatting about what they’d learned from the talks and the ideas they wanted to try. It was great to hear that enthusiasm! I was already playing around with project ideas myself, using tools or tech stacks I hadn’t tried before.

One perk of being a room support volunteer was getting to enjoy the talks myself while on duty. We also had time off to explore the partner stands, chat with the people there, and do a bit of networking ourselves.

Those chats brought out my talkative side, which I found kind of amusing. I’m usually more of an introvert, but it was just so easy to talk to people here! I ended up exchanging contact details with a few of the TDC partners. We’ll see if that helps with my job hunt, but either way, I really enjoyed getting to know them.

After the conference, we had a small afterparty with food and drinks. It was nice to finally wind down together and chat about how the day went. We also got chatting about work and life outside the conference, and I really enjoyed getting to know my team a little better. Sadly, I didn’t manage to say goodbye to everyone before we went our separate ways, but we added each other on LinkedIn. Hopefully, I’ll see some familiar faces next year if we get the chance to volunteer again!

# Takeaways

So far in my career, I haven’t had many opportunities to see how people approach problems in complex software systems and platforms. I really enjoyed the talks that went into just enough depth for me to understand the challenges engineers and consultants face, and how they work through them.

Volunteering can also get you into a conference when the ticket price would otherwise put it out of reach. And you get to meet people and have a chat along the way.

I really enjoyed being part of the community around the conference. Everyone was so nice, patient, and genuinely interesting to talk to. Some of those conversations have already turned into LinkedIn exchanges and follow-ups that I’m still working through as I write this!

There’s a human side to all of this that’s easy to overlook. We spend so much time interacting through screens, especially in tech. I’ve got to say, there’s something special about being in the same room as people who get excited about the same things you do. It makes you feel part of something bigger in a way a virtual event can’t quite replace.

People need other people. I think events like this, and the number of volunteers they attract, show that we haven’t forgotten that!

All in all, I had so much fun, and I’d happily do TDC again next year! 🐤
